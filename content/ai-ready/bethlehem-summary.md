## 1. Short Overview

Bethlehem is one of the oldest historic cities in Palestine, about 10 km south of
Jerusalem, on a plateau roughly 775 m above sea level. It is world-famous because, in
Christian tradition, it is the birthplace of Jesus Christ, and it is visited by millions
of pilgrims and tourists every year. Alongside its religious standing, it has thousands
of years of civilisational history — Canaanite, Roman, Byzantine, Muslim Arab, Crusader,
Mamluk, Ottoman, then the British Mandate and the modern era. It is bordered by
Jerusalem to the north, Beit Sahour to the east, Beit Jala to the west, and al-Khader to
the south-west. The governorate covers roughly 608 km².

## 2. Key Historical Timeline

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| Canaanite era (2nd millennium BCE) | Archaeological finds show Bethlehem was inhabited from the 2nd millennium BCE, founded by Canaanites who built fortified towns and trading centres. It sat on old trade routes linking Jerusalem and Hebron to the south, and lived on agriculture, olive and vine cultivation, and sheep herding. |
| Name origin | The name comes from Canaanite. *Bayt* means house or place; *Lahm / Lakhm* is linked by some researchers to an ancient Canaanite deity named Lakhm, while other readings give "house of bread" in the Semitic languages. |
| 4th century CE (Byzantine) | Emperor Constantine I and his mother Helena ordered the building of the Church of the Nativity; it was completed around 339 CE. |
| 6th century CE | Emperor Justinian I rebuilt parts of the church after it was damaged. |
| Crusader era (from 1099 CE) | Bethlehem became part of the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem. The Crusaders restored the Church of the Nativity and decorated it with mosaics and wall paintings, and built monasteries and pilgrim facilities. The city became a main stop on the Christian pilgrimage route. |
| Ayyubid era (1187–1250 CE) | After Salah al-Din's victory at Hattin in 1187 CE, Bethlehem came under Ayyubid rule. The Church of the Nativity kept its religious role and Christian pilgrims continued to visit; the holy places were respected. |
| Mamluk era (1250–1517 CE) | The city retained its religious standing and continued to receive pilgrims. Restoration work was carried out on religious buildings and access roads, and pilgrimage routes were secured, supporting trade. |
| Ottoman era (1517–1917 CE) | About four centuries of relative stability. Agriculture flourished — olives, vines and almonds — and the olive-wood craft industry developed into the city's best-known traditional industry. The Church of the Nativity underwent several restorations. |
| British Mandate (1917–1948) | Roads and services improved; the city continued its religious and tourist role while being affected by the political events of the period. |
| Modern era | After 1948 Bethlehem came under Jordanian administration; in 1967 it became part of the territory occupied by Israel; since 1995 it has been administered by the Palestinian National Authority under the agreements signed at that time. |
| 2012 | The Church of the Nativity and the Pilgrimage Route were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. |
| 2019 | The site was removed from the List of World Heritage in Danger after the required conservation work was completed. |

## 3. Cultural and Religious Importance

- **Christianity:** one of the holiest Christian cities, linked in Christian tradition to
  the birth of Jesus Christ. Pilgrims visit the Church of the Nativity, the Grotto of the
  Nativity, the Pilgrimage Route and the Shepherds' Fields. Its Christmas celebrations
  are among the best known religious celebrations in the world.
- **Islam:** Bethlehem holds a special place for Muslims as the city where the Prophet
  Isa (Jesus) was born according to Islamic tradition. The Mosque of Omar is its most
  prominent Islamic landmark and is regarded as a symbol of the religious coexistence the
  city has been known for.
- **Culture:** a model of Palestinian heritage — traditional crafts, music, festivals,
  embroidery, and olive-wood carving, making it a centre for preserving Palestinian
  cultural identity.
- **Crafts:** olive-wood objects (crosses, religious figures, rosaries, decorated boxes,
  souvenirs); mother-of-pearl work used on crosses, frames, boxes and icons; and
  Palestinian embroidery of traditional dresses.
- **Food heritage:** musakhan, maqluba, maftoul, mansaf, kubbeh, stuffed vine leaves,
  ma'moul, Jerusalem ka'ak, and kunafa.
- **Customs:** welcoming guests with Arabic coffee, exchanging food during holidays,
  decorating Manger Square at Christmas, religious processions and popular celebrations,
  and passing traditional crafts between generations.

## 4. Main Landmarks

- **Church of the Nativity** — completed around 339 CE under Constantine I and Helena,
  partly rebuilt by Justinian I in the 6th century CE. Known for its massive stone
  columns, ancient mosaic floors, historic icons, wooden roof, and the Grotto of the
  Nativity with its fourteen-pointed silver star. One of the oldest churches still in
  use for worship; UNESCO World Heritage listed in 2012.
- **Milk Grotto** — a short distance from the Church of the Nativity.
- **Shepherds' Fields** — in the neighbouring town of Beit Sahour; today it holds small
  churches, archaeological caves and pilgrim facilities.
- **Mosque of Omar** — in Manger Square directly opposite the Church of the Nativity;
  built in the 19th century CE and named after Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab.
- **Solomon's Pools** — south-west of Bethlehem; three large pools used since antiquity
  to collect rain and spring water. Archaeological study indicates they were created and
  developed in the Roman period and used in the Islamic and Ottoman periods to supply
  Jerusalem and Bethlehem with water.
- **The old market** — small shops selling heritage products and handicrafts.

## 5. Important Facts

- Elevation: approximately 775 m above sea level.
- Roughly 10 km south of Jerusalem.
- Governorate area: about 608 km².
- The Church of the Nativity is one of the oldest churches in the world still used for
  worship.
- The Church of the Nativity and the Pilgrimage Route were World Heritage listed in 2012
  and removed from the danger list in 2019.
- Known for olive-wood and mother-of-pearl crafts.
- Hosts annual Christmas celebrations that draw visitors from around the world.

## 6. Religious Traditions (NOT to be presented as historical fact)

- Christian tradition holds that the Church of the Nativity was built over the grotto
  where Jesus was born, and the fourteen-pointed silver star marks that place.
- Christian tradition holds that the Virgin Mary sheltered in the Milk Grotto with the
  infant Jesus before the journey to Egypt.
- Christian tradition holds that the angel appeared to the shepherds in the Shepherds'
  Fields announcing the birth of Christ.
- Islamic tradition regards Bethlehem as the birthplace of the Prophet Isa.

## 7. Oral Heritage and Popular Stories (NOT historical fact)

- **The Milk Grotto legend** — a popular account says a drop of the Virgin Mary's milk
  fell on the rocks and turned them white, giving the grotto its name. Thousands of
  pilgrims visit each year, especially couples praying for children. The source file
  states explicitly that this account is part of religious and popular tradition and is
  **not** an established historical fact.
- **Solomon's Pools naming** — popular heritage attributes the pools to the Prophet
  Solomon, but archaeological studies date their creation and development to the Roman
  period.
