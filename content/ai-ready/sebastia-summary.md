## 1. Short Overview

Sebastia is one of the most important archaeological and historical sites in Palestine.
It lies about 12 km north-west of Nablus on a hill roughly 440 m above sea level,
overlooking the surrounding plains and valleys — a position that made it politically,
commercially and militarily important across many eras. Successive civilisations left
remains on the same site: Canaanite, ancient Israelite, Assyrian, Greek, Roman,
Byzantine, Islamic, Crusader and Ottoman. It is bordered by Nablus to the south-east,
Jenin to the north, and the villages of Zawata, al-Naqura and Burqa. The area has a
Mediterranean climate and is surrounded by farmland known for olive, almond and fig trees.

## 2. Key Historical Timeline

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| Canaanite era (2nd millennium BCE) | Archaeological evidence shows the area was inhabited from the 2nd millennium BCE by Canaanites, who used the elevated position to protect the town and watch the trade routes. They lived on agriculture, livestock, trade, and water storage in wells and cisterns. |
| 9th century BCE | The ancient Canaanite name of the city was Shomron (Samaria); it was the capital of the northern Kingdom of Israel. |
| 27 BCE | Herod the Great rebuilt the city and named it Sebastia — the Greek equivalent of the title of the Roman emperor Augustus — in his honour. The name "Samaria" remained in use in some historical and religious sources. |
| Roman era | Major construction: the colonnaded street, the theatre, and the Temple of Augustus built by Herod the Great. |
| Byzantine era | The city prospered; excavations revealed mosaic floors, columns, and remains of religious buildings. A cathedral was built at the site associated with John the Baptist. |
| Islamic conquest (638 CE) | Sebastia came under Islamic rule during the conquest of the Levant under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab. It remained inhabited, existing buildings were largely preserved, and new buildings were added. |
| Crusader era (1099–1187 CE) | The Crusaders rebuilt a large church over the site attributed to the tomb of John the Baptist, and built defensive structures and towers. The town became a stop for European pilgrims; remains of the Crusader church still stand. |
| Ayyubid and Mamluk eras | After Salah al-Din al-Ayyubi restored Muslim rule, parts of the Crusader church were converted during the Mamluk period into the Mosque of Nabi Yahya, keeping much of the original building. The town continued as an agricultural village. |
| Ottoman era (1517–1917 CE) | Sebastia became a small Palestinian village living on olives and olive oil, figs, grapes and almonds, and sheep and goat herding. Its Roman and Byzantine remains continued to attract European travellers and researchers who described them in their books. |
| Modern era | After Ottoman rule the area came under the British Mandate, then Jordanian administration after 1948. Since 1967 it has been within the territory occupied by Israel, while the Palestinian town is administered in civil matters by the Palestinian National Authority, with administrative complications tied to the archaeological site. |

## 3. Cultural and Religious Importance

- Sebastia is a living record of the civilisations that succeeded one another in
  Palestine, gathering Canaanite, ancient Israelite, Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, Crusader,
  Mamluk and Ottoman remains in a single site.
- It reflects overlap between cultures and religions, holding both Islamic and Christian
  religious remains at the same location.
- The Cathedral of St John / Mosque of Nabi Yahya is significant to both Christians and
  Muslims, being associated with the Prophet Yahya (John the Baptist in Christian
  tradition), who holds a special place in Islam.
- **Crafts:** pottery (large quantities of vessels were uncovered in excavations), stone
  carving using local limestone, Palestinian embroidery, and olive oil production using
  both traditional and modern methods.
- **Agriculture:** olives, grapes, figs, almonds, wheat and barley. Olive groves extend
  around the archaeological site and form part of the landscape.
- **Food heritage:** musakhan, maqluba, maftoul, mansaf, mujaddara, kubbeh, ma'moul, and
  local za'atar with olive oil.
- **Customs:** welcoming guests with Arabic coffee, cooperating during the olive harvest,
  hosting feasts at social occasions, passing crafts between generations, and taking part
  in cultural festivals that introduce the town's history and heritage.

## 4. Main Landmarks

- **The Roman colonnaded street** — the best-known monument at the site; a long street
  flanked by two rows of massive stone columns that carried porticoes used by merchants
  and passers-by. It was the city's commercial and administrative centre.
- **The Roman theatre** — on the eastern side of the city, built in the Roman period for
  performances, celebrations and public assemblies; semi-circular with stone seating.
- **The Temple of Augustus** — built by Herod the Great in honour of Augustus; one of the
  largest temples in the region, now surviving only as foundations and some columns.
- **Cathedral of St John / Mosque of Nabi Yahya** — built in the Byzantine period, rebuilt
  by the Crusaders, and partly converted into the Mosque of Nabi Yahya in the Islamic
  period.
- **The royal tombs** — rock-cut tombs; archaeologists attribute some to the ancient
  Israelite period and others to the Roman and Byzantine periods. They have stone
  entrances, burial chambers and areas for funerary rites.
- **Walls and towers** — defensive walls rebuilt and expanded in several eras, especially
  the Hellenistic and Roman periods, with watchtowers overlooking the trade routes and
  surrounding plains.
- **The old markets** — historically sold grain, olive oil, grapes, pottery, metal tools,
  fabrics and produce; today small shops sell souvenirs, Palestinian embroidery, olive
  oil, Nabulsi soap, and pieces inspired by the Roman remains.

## 5. Important Facts

- About 12 km north-west of Nablus.
- Situated on a hill roughly 440 m above sea level.
- Was the capital of the northern Kingdom of Israel in the 9th century BCE.
- Herod the Great rebuilt the city and named it Sebastia in honour of Emperor Augustus.
- Holds one of the best-preserved Roman streets in Palestine.
- Contains remains of a Roman temple, a theatre, a Byzantine church, a historic mosque,
  walls and archaeological tombs.
- Conservation challenges include erosion and weather damage, ongoing restoration needs,
  protecting the site from encroachment, funding for excavation, and managing cultural
  tourism. The Palestinian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities works with international
  institutions and archaeologists on conservation and documentation.

## 6. Religious Traditions (NOT to be presented as historical fact)

- The site of the Cathedral of St John is *attributed* to the tomb of John the Baptist
  (the Prophet Yahya in Islam); the source describes this as an attribution, not an
  archaeologically established fact.
- The Prophet Yahya holds a special standing in Islam, which is why the site attracted
  Muslim attention as well as Christian.

## 7. Oral Heritage and Popular Stories (NOT historical fact)

- Local cultural festivals retell the town's history among the Roman columns; these
  retellings are heritage practice rather than primary historical evidence.
- The continued use of the older name "Samaria" alongside "Sebastia" in religious and
  historical sources is itself part of the town's transmitted memory.
